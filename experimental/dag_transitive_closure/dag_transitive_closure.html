Step scratch packed by the DAG's closure

An experiment for the Rust redesign's step scratch: does packing each step's scratch region from the transitive closure of the step DAG behave as the design says? A model over a fixed dummy DAG, on this page in JavaScript and in Python (model.py), which is the reference: it carries the same values, checks the page's input against its own, and pins every number the page prints.

uv run python experimental/dag_transitive_closure/model.py   # standard library, ~17 s

The question

The redesign keeps Outputs summed and packs only step scratch: memory a step needs during its run and nothing reads afterwards. A step declares its region's size; once every step has, the final budget node takes the closure of the DAG and packs the regions into one slab per device and kind. Two regions may overlap only if one step reaches the other in the DAG. A finished node is finished device work, so a path is a happens-before on the card too, and no edge is ever added for memory.

What the model has to show:

  1. The packing is safe: every pair of overlapping regions is ordered by the closure.
  2. It is deterministic: the offsets depend on the DAG and the sizes, never on the order steps declared in.
  3. It is good: its peak against the lower bound, the heaviest set of mutually unordered regions, which no packing that keeps unordered regions apart can beat; and against the two designs it replaces, the plain sum and one max per device under a lock.
  4. Its limits are the ones the design names: a chain packs to its largest region; a fully parallel DAG packs to the sum; and a peak inside one step is not lowered.

The model

Twelve steps, one node each, shaped like a DF-RHF setup followed by a CASSCF and an NEVPT2. An edge is a declared read. Every size is a dummy value in MiB, loosely gly10-shaped so the proportions mean something (B's 7.8 GB slab, the three-centre step's work lists and build intermediates at about 2 GB), and none is a measurement. One device, one kind; packing is per device and kind, so one is enough to test it.

The closure

Steps in a topological order; each step's set of descendants is the union of its successors and their descendants, taken in reverse order. At a few dozen steps a bitset per step is all it needs. Two steps are ordered when either reaches the other.

ordered: regions may overlap unordered: regions kept apart

The packing

Greedy first fit, in a fixed order: largest region first, ties by step id. Each region goes at the lowest offset where it overlaps no already-placed region of a step it is unordered with. Regions of ordered steps are ignored, so they may land on the same bytes. The order depends only on the sizes and the steps' ids, not on the order they were declared in, so the offsets are the same every run.

for r in sorted(regions, key=lambda r: (-r.size, r.id)):
    conflicts = [q for q in placed if not ordered(r, q)]
    r.offset = lowest o in {0} ∪ {q.offset + q.size} with
               [o, o + r.size) disjoint from every conflict
    placed.append(r)
peak = max(r.offset + r.size)

What the dummy DAG says

    What model.py checks

    • Safety: for every pair of regions whose byte ranges intersect, the closure orders the two steps. On this DAG and on random DAGs.
    • Determinism: the offsets are identical when the steps are declared in any order (every permutation of a small DAG, shuffles of a large one).
    • The page's numbers: the sum, the max, the packed peak, the lower bound and every offset printed here, so page and model cannot drift.
    • Limits: a chain packs to its largest region; a DAG with no edges packs to the sum; on every DAG, max ≤ lower bound ≤ packed ≤ sum.
    • A replayed loop body: the closure is taken over one iteration's body, computed once; two steps unordered within the body stay apart.
    • Packing quality: the packed peak against the lower bound on random DAGs, to see how far first fit falls short, if at all.

    Those hold the packing against the closure, which shows the packer obeys the closure and cannot show the closure is the right rule. Added on 2026-11-21:

    • The closure itself, against plain depth-first reachability: an independent computation, where the page's JavaScript copies the same bitset logic.
    • Execution: simulated runs on a pool of 1 to 8 workers, any ready step taken at random as stealing would, each step with a random host part and device part. A region is in use from its step's start to the end of its device work. No two regions sharing bytes may ever be in use at once.
    • The negative control: the same runs with the step-end sync dropped, so a node finishes when its host part does and its device work runs on. The simulation must now find collisions; if it did not, it would be testing nothing.
    • Devices and kinds: one slab per (device, kind), packed independently; a multi-GPU step declaring on both cards; pinned staging beside device memory.
    • A manager's scratch: declared by each caller, as the redesign proposes, against owned by the manager.
    • A condition's exclusive branches: what the closure does with steps that never run together.
    • Better packing: first fit over a fixed set of four orders (largest, most constrained, size × conflicts, topological), keeping the lowest peak; and the exact optimum, by first fit over every order, on small DAGs.

    Exact optimum, 4000 small DAGsAt most 8 regions, small integer sizes: the optimum equals the lower bound on every one. Largest first is optimal on 3769, the best of four orders on 3953
    • The step-end sync is load-bearing, and now shown to be. With it no run collides; without it four runs in five do. The closure is the right rule exactly because a finished node is finished device work.
    • A manager must not own scratch. Callers declaring it, sized by the manager's size function, is the only form of the two that is safe. That settles the redesign's proposal on correctness, not only on tidiness.
    • Exclusive branches are a possible saving, not adopted. The closure is conservative there and safe. Treating two branches as exclusive is safe only if rustflow never runs both, which holds for one condition's successors but needs the condition's semantics stated; it would be the first rule beyond the DAG itself.
    • Use the best of the four orders. Deterministic, since the set is fixed; it costs four first-fit passes at a few dozen steps, nothing next to budget mode's allocations; it cuts the misses from 56 to 24 and the worst case from 1.28 to 1.17.
    • The lower bound is not always reachable in principle. It was on every DAG the model could solve exactly. But this is dynamic storage allocation over the conflicts, which is NP-hard even when lifetimes are intervals, while the bound (a heaviest clique of a perfect graph) is cheap; so, unless P = NP, instances above it exist. None appeared at this size.

    Out of scope: rustflow itself (the simulation models its pool, not its code), a device, real sizes, and packing Outputs over a producer-to-last-reader interval (the same closure, held until the measurement says Outputs are what stacks).
