Rust rebuildProgramming model

We design a feature as a DAG of tasks before we write it as code. A node is a task. An edge is data that one task produces and another consumes. The orchestrator admits, places, schedules and joins the nodes. The tasks only compute.

The model is not new to this tree. The C++ pipeline is already an instance of it, in its most restricted form, and the first section says exactly which restrictions the Rust rebuild lifts. The rest of the document is the contract: what a task declares, what an edge declares, what the orchestrator derives, and how one DAG runs unchanged on one GPU, on a node of GPUs, and across nodes.

The pipeline is already an instance

A C++ pipeline step declares its memory footprint as a formula in the plan's dimensions, footprint(dims). The plan fold, then over the steps in plan order with drop for what each one frees, finds the peak and the step that reaches it, and check_plan refuses the plan against the [memory] caps before the first step runs (src/pipeline/footprint). Steps run one at a time, on one device.

Two things are fixed there that the model leaves free:

  • The schedule is a list. The plan order is the config's order. Formally the steps are a DAG: the overlap and the core Hamiltonian share no edge, and either may run first. A step is itself a sub-DAG, and some of those are replayed: the RHF step runs one iteration's DAG until convergence.
  • The placement is one device. Every step runs on the one card the plan was given.

The rebuild keeps the contract and relaxes both: the list becomes a DAG, and the device becomes a topology. That relaxation also says what the orchestrator's first job is, on one GPU: not running nodes in parallel, but finding an order whose peak fits the budget. Admission and scheduling are one problem, not two. Parallelism is whatever room the chosen order leaves.

Steps on rustflow

Two layers are in the tree, as in memory.md. The v0.0.4 layer (fb_core_flow, over fb_core_budget and fb_core_device) is this section, the design the v0.0.4 prototype was built to try; its streams, locks and handles are gpu_layer.md's, "The v0.0.4 runtime". The old layer, tasks, edges and the orchestrator in fb_core_planner and fb_core_thread_pool, is every section from "Three owners" on, until its crates are deleted; "CPU workers" and "Capabilities are enforced by lints" say what holds for both.

A step is one config table, one slot, mem_budget and run. A Step is a unit struct named by value. Its Output is what outlives its run, behind a lock used plainly: the owner writes, a reader reads. Its mem_budget allocates the Output through a Budget, declares its scratch and the locks its run takes, and reads only what setup filled (memory.md, "The budget"). Its run is handed the Output, a View, the guards the wrapper took (Held), its streams (Lane) and its scratch (Lease), and no Budget and no bus.

The declaration is the edge. A step names the steps it reads as a tuple, Step::Reads. The builder makes the DAG's edges from those tuples and nothing else, and View::read compiles only for a step in the reader's tuple, so a read with no edge behind it does not build. A step the config does not ask for is not in the DAG, and Dag::build refuses a step that reads one, naming both, before anything is budgeted; so is a step added twice, and a cycle.

One graph, two modes. Plan::budget runs every step's mem_budget over the DAG's edges on rustflow's Executor, then one final node, Ledger::finish (memory.md, "The final node"). Plan::execute runs every run over the same edges, allocates nothing budgeted, and may be called again: a replay writes into the same buffers and the same scratch regions. A step's inner work is plain code inside its run: rustflow schedules the outer graph and nothing inside a step.

The wrapper. Every run is entered and left by one function a step cannot skip:

  1. Make the step's device current: gpu0's for a GPU step, and each thread of a fan-out makes its own GPU's current.
  2. Take the locks its mem_budget declared, in rank order (LockId's derived order: every manager, then the GPUs by index), and bind a GPU's handle workspace the first time its lock is taken.
  3. In the ledger's NaN mode, fill the lease: its host regions, and its device regions on the step's first stream on each GPU, synced.
  4. Call run with the held guards.
  5. Synchronize every stream the step has and each held GPU's stream, failed or not.
  6. Release the locks.

So a finished node is finished device work, and the edges order everything between steps with no event between them. Each execute records every step's acquisitions in its StepTime: the locks in the order taken, whether one was found held so that the step waited, and when it held them all and when it began to release them. Nothing takes a lock inside run: a step reaches a GPU's handles only through the guards in its Held, and Locks gives no path to a Mutex; its one locking method, query, takes a Budget, which only mem_budget holds. A step that declares a GPU's lock it does not run on is refused in budget mode.

A failed step fails its successors and nothing else. The wrapper keeps a failed flag per node; a node with a failed predecessor is skipped and flagged in turn, and the plan returns the first failure in its one topological order, the others on its context (diagnostics.md, "One worker fails").

The MainBus and its steps

fb-steps is the run's bus and its steps, in one crate so the bus names every step's Output with no dependency cycle; the method code a step calls stays in its module's crate.

  • The config is one table per step family. [molecule] and [basis] bring the overlap, the core Hamiltonian, the guess and the orthogonalizer; [eri] the metric and the ERI; [scf] the SCF; [localization] the localization of the SCF's occupied orbitals; [resources] the GPUs, the executor's width and the caps. A step whose table is absent is not in the DAG, and a step that reads one the config leaves out is refused.
  • calculation picks the steps and the products. "overlap" is the overlap alone, "guess" the guess alone, "rhf" the chain to the SCF, "localization" that and the localization; the optional tables it reads are filled at their defaults as the config is parsed, so the rule above still places each step. A table it does not read is refused at the table's line, and "rhf" or "localization" with no [eri] at calculation's, each naming the file (InvalidConfig). The free-breakfast binary is fb-steps' own: a config through run, its products written by Run::write, its main the one boundary (diagnostics.md, "The one boundary").
  • [guess] chooses the guess's density, the SAD density when absent. from loads a float64 .npy (a run's d.npy) into the guess's staging in place of the SAD stamps, so its budget is the SAD guess's. Setup refuses it beside type or of a shape other than n_ao × n_ao (InvalidConfig), and with an open shell (Unsupported: d.npy is ROHF's two spin densities summed, which cannot restart it), before anything is allocated, naming the key, the file and the line.
  • [molecule] sets the charge and the multiplicity, and setup counts the occupations. charge (default 0) and multiplicity (default 1) give Dims' n_alpha and n_beta, n_alpha - n_beta = multiplicity - 1, beside n_occ, the doubly occupied, n_beta. A multiplicity the electron count cannot have is refused InvalidConfig, naming the key, the file and the line (documentation.md, "Configuration: the reference is emitted from the type"); an open shell, n_alpha > n_beta, runs ROHF in the SCF. A mem_budget over the AO space reads the occupations only for whether the shell is open (the SCF's open-shell scratch, the J/K pass's second density), so its budget for a closed-shell ion is the neutral's. A step whose arrays span the occupied space, the localization, sizes them by its blocks of C's columns, [0, n_beta) and [n_beta, n_alpha): an n_ao bound would cost it (n_ao / n_occ)², 22 times at gly10, in its population tensor.
  • Setup is the unbudgeted host phase, and it counts. MainBus::setup reads the geometry and both bases, fills Dims by a walk over the shells that builds no array (for_each_pair), makes each GPU's GpuResources before it reads the device caps, and returns the bus. Every mem_budget is then a function of Dims, the step's options and the caps, so a dry budget, with no device behind it, prints exact lengths: only a solver's work buffer, which needs a live handle to query, is counted as zero. A GPU's lock is declared and checked in a dry plan too, against the plan's GPUs rather than the devices behind them; a host-backed plan refuses to run a step that takes one.
  • A host step builds on the host and uploads. S, H = T + V, (P|Q) and the SAD density are built on rayon's threads ("CPU workers"), or a loaded guess read from its file, written into the step's pinned scratch and uploaded on the step's own stream, each copy's token dropped, since the staging is a tenant of the lease and the wrapper's step-end sync covers the copy. A copy holds its host end until a sync covers it, so a step that uploads to every GPU stages once per GPU. A host step takes no lock and runs beside the GPU work.
  • A library step takes its GPU's lock and enqueues on the guard's stream. The orthogonalizer, X = S^(-1/2), runs syevd, the inverse square root, a dgmm and a gemm on gpu0's guarded stream, with no event, its eigenvectors and work buffer in its device scratch; it reads the solver's flag on the host after its last enqueue (gpu_layer.md, "The v0.0.4 runtime").
  • The SCF is one all-GPU step whose loop is plain code in run. Each iteration is the EriManager's build_jk over a factor of the carried density (its occupied orbitals, or on a fresh run's first pass the guess's eigen-factor above a floor), F, the energy, the residual, the DIIS step and the Roothaan step, on gpu0's guarded stream in its scratch; the turn's scalars are read on the host through their download's token once, after the turn's last enqueue. An open shell's iteration is ROHF's: one J/K pass over its alpha and beta factors, the spin Fock matrices, and the Roothaan effective Fock matrix in place of F, on which the residual, the DIIS step and the Roothaan step are the closed shell's; its scratch follows the closed shell's in the one layout, and its Output adds the effective Fock matrix, the occupation classes C's columns [0, n_beta) and [n_beta, n_alpha). fb_steps::run is setup, budget mode and execute mode over the run's one executor, and Run::replay execute mode again in the same buffers: the SCF's orbitals carry over in its Output, nothing in scratch does.
  • The localization is a gpu0 step under gpu0's lock whose sweep loop is plain code in run. It reads the overlap's S and the SCF's C and orbital energies, and rotates each block of occupied columns by Pipek-Mezey sweeps on the population tensor; each turn reads L, the turn's orbitals C U and the sort key diag(Uᵀ diag(ε) U), each column's ⟨i|F|i⟩, through their downloads' token, the loop's turn read. The converged turn's columns are stably sorted by the key, on the host copy and on the device. An open shell's two blocks, the doubly and the singly occupied columns, are each localized among itself and sorted by its own key, one after the other in the step's scratch, and L and the sweeps are summed over them.

The EriManager

The density-fitted ERI is a shared product with state of its own: streams and events, and a ring of buffers on every GPU. That state lives in one object, fb_steps::EriManager, which is eri's Output, and its users call its methods. build_jk(shared, held, d, j, k) is the one that works: J and K for each density of d, on gpu0, into the caller's j and k.

  • It is eri's Output. eri's mem_budget allocates all of it: the metric's Cholesky factor L on every GPU, B's slab on the host heap, and on every GPU the ERI buffer and its pinned staging (memory.md, "The budget"). They are summed with every other Output, for the whole calculation. eri's run factors the metric, builds B and fills the manager.
  • Its readers declare it, like any Output. The edge comes from the declaration. A reader that calls build_jk also declares the manager's lock and every GPU's, and the wrapper takes them, the manager's first. B and L are immutable after eri; the per-GPU state changes only under the manager's lock.
  • It never takes a lock. The guards it uses come in held, taken by the wrapper, and it has no path to a GPU's Mutex. Its own state sits behind a Mutex that is only ever try_locked, under the manager's lock: a caller that did not declare that lock panics there, and fails the method's contract first in a debug build.
  • The caller owns its results. build_jk writes the J and K it is passed, reads the densities it is passed, and keeps nothing of the caller's. A caller's own state (its DIIS, its Fock matrix) stays in the caller's scratch.
  • It takes a slice of densities. DF-RHF passes one, and ROHF two, its alpha and beta factors, in one pass: each aux batch is uploaded and gathered once, and contracted with each density in turn. A response solve, a Davidson or a state average would pass many. The buffers are sized for an open shell's two, a closed shell's one, from Dims, a contract of build_jk.
  • A density is D, or a factor C of D = 2 C Cᵀ (Density::Dense, Density::Occupied). From D, K costs two n_aux × n_ao³ products; from C, two n_aux × n_ao² × n_occ ones, and J comes from the same half-transformed B. A caller that has a factor passes it: the SCF does on every pass, its first the guess's eigen-factor. Only the matrix given crosses to the other GPUs: downloaded from gpu0 once, and one host end uploaded to each of them under one token.

B is built over pairs and contracted over aux functions. A pair column of B depends on every P, so a batch of P cannot be fitted alone; a batch of pairs can. The pair batches, each a run of one class's pairs, partition the pair columns, and each GPU takes a contiguous range of them: it forms (μν|P) in its ring, fits it against its own L, downloads it and scatters it into its own columns of every row of B, which split_at_mut cut from the other GPUs', so no GPU needs anything from another and no pointer is shared. A J/K pass then runs over aux batches: the aux functions are split across the GPUs once, in budget mode, each GPU contracts its own share, and the fragments are summed onto gpu0 through the host.

(μν|P) is spherical from the kernel. FelizJueves's ssperi3<la, lb, lP> writes all three indices as real solid harmonics, each pair's block at its spherical pair column, the layout the fit reads. No bra transform runs, and the ring holds no Cartesian batch and no transform scratch. It is plain FP64. No array in the DF-RHF chain has a Cartesian index.

The build is Schwarz-screened. [eri] schwarz_threshold, 1e-12 by default (the old path's), drops a shell pair whose bound is under it: the pair has no cells, the ring buffer it would have been written into is zeroed first, and its columns of B are zero. The bound is the sum of the pair's records' dressed Schwarz bounds, formed on each GPU from the records it already holds (fb_integrals_sys::record_bound) and read on the host once, before the first batch; it is at least sqrt((mn|mn)) for every Cartesian m, n of the pair, and the fit is a projection, Σ_Q B_Q,mn² <= (mn|mn), so a dropped column is that small. B and the ring stay at the unscreened bound Dims gives (memory.md, "Batched only"), so the budget does not depend on the threshold, and 0 is the unscreened build.

The build's ring. [eri] ring pair batches are in flight per GPU, two or one. Each ring buffer has a stream of its own, which carries its batch's cells, its three-centre kernels and its download. The fit, a blocked triangular solve, goes on the GPU's stream: it waits on the event the ring stream recorded after the kernels, and the download waits on the event recorded after the fit. The host waits on a buffer's download token, which syncs that buffer's stream alone, before it scatters the batch into B and writes the buffer's pinned bytes again (gpu_layer.md, "The v0.0.4 runtime").

A J/K pass uploads its whole share, through a ring of the same depth. B has one copy, on the host; no GPU keeps rows of it between passes. Each aux batch goes through one of the pass's ring buffers, each with a pinned end and an upload stream of the manager's own:

  1. The host copies the batch's rows of B into the buffer's pinned end, once the upload that last read those bytes has run.
  2. The upload, and the gather of the packed rows into square matrices, go on the buffer's stream, after the event the GPU's stream recorded behind the contraction that last read the buffer.
  3. The contraction goes on the GPU's stream, after the event the buffer's stream recorded behind the gather.

So a batch's host copy and its upload both run while the batch before is contracted, and the GPU never waits for the host. The pass joins its streams before it returns: the wrapper does not know the manager's streams. With the ring a pass takes the contraction's time, so nothing is gained by keeping rows of B on a GPU.

The ERI buffer. One device buffer per GPU within [eri] mem_per_gpu, eri's step limit, charged at what it uses, with its pinned staging. eri's build and every later J/K pass run in it, one after the other, since the DAG orders eri before every reader: during eri's run it holds the build's ring; afterwards a pass's gather tables, a copy of the density, the J and K fragments, one aux batch's work and the pass's ring. It is held for the whole calculation, so what it takes is not available to later steps, and the budget table shows it in eri's row.

Pinned staging does not grow with the limit. A fitted pair batch comes down through a staging buffer of at most 64 MiB, and an aux batch of B's rows goes up through one of at most 128 MiB. Neither batch is ever wider than its staging buffer holds, whatever mem_per_gpu is: a batch is one copy. Page-locking costs about 0.9 s per GiB, and each ring page-locks a buffer for each of its depth, while the build's time is flat in the width well below 64 MiB (memory.md, "Batched only"). So the buffer stops growing with the limit.

Derivatives of B are scratch. A gradient or a Hessian step builds the derivative of B a batch at a time and contracts it at once, inside the step that needs it. It reads the metric and L, which is why the metric is an Output of its own and L is the manager's. No such step is built yet.

What the model covers

The layer runs the DF-RHF chain batched on one to four GPUs, with B on the host. What follows is the argument that the same model carries the rest of the C++ tree: each of its constraints, against what src/ needs. None of it past the chain is built.

The model holds What src/ needs
Every length is counted before anything runs Already so: src/pipeline/shape and footprint size every step from its dimensions at plan time, AVAS included. A size known only at run time is allocated at its bound (memory.md, "Batched only")
One config table, one slot, one instance per step The C++ step contract (kTable, kKeys). Two states' gradients for an MECI, or a QD pair's, live inside one step
Nothing is allocated in run Steps sized from footprints already allocate up front
A library-using step holds its GPUs for its whole run Two heavy steps on one GPU time-slice anyway
Every GPU step is batched and spans every GPU A cost of each port, not a limit of the model

Repeated evaluations are replays. Finite-difference gradients and Hessians, geometry optimization, IRC, and molecular dynamics over a fixed atom list, basis and active space run execute mode again at the same Dims, in the same buffers, each from the orbitals of the evaluation before. Displacements run one after another: every heavy step already spans every GPU, so a serial replay leaves none idle. Concurrent replays would need copies of the chain's buffers and pay only on molecules too small to fill the GPUs; none is in scope.

A calculation whose Dims change mid-run is a new DAG per segment: a QM/MM run whose QM region moves, an active space that changes along a trajectory. Each segment pays setup and budget mode, seconds against its run.

The sum is bounded by batching. Every O(N³) tensor is batched under a step limit, so what stays alive as an Output is O(N²) matrices (32 MB each at n_ao = 2000) and active-space tensors (a 3-RDM at 16 orbitals is 134 MB). A Hessian's per-perturbation response, 3 n_atom × n_ao × n_occ, is its step's scratch, batched over perturbations, and does not stack. A chain the sum refuses has a remedy within the model: Outputs packed by the same closure that packs scratch.

Vocabulary

Each word below means one thing in the v0.0.4 layer's sections of these documents.

The calculation

Word Means
step One node of the outer graph: a type with mem_budget and run, one config table and one slot. Something is its own step only if another step reads its Output, its Output carries over a replay, or it has a config table of its own
inner task A unit of a step's own work inside run: one GPU's thread of a fan-out, one turn of its loop. Plain code, not a rustflow task, and not a step
node A node of the outer graph; one per step, plus budget mode's final node
wrapper The one generic function around every step's mem_budget and run: it makes the device current, takes the step's locks, calls the body, syncs, and releases
Output What a step leaves on the MainBus: what other steps read, or what carries over a replay
MainBus The one shared object: the config, Dims, the caps and counters, each GPU's GpuResources, and one slot per step
slot A step's RwLock<Option<Output>> on the MainBus. Used only in this sense
View What run receives in place of the bus: the shared setup and the Outputs the step declared it reads, and nothing else
Dims Every count setup makes: n_atom, n_ao, n_aux, n_occ, and every array length a step needs
setup Before the DAG: parse the config, the geometry and the bases on the host, fill Dims, make each GPU's GpuResources. Not budgeted
budget mode The DAG run once with each node calling mem_budget: count, check, allocate. Then the final node
final node The last node of budget mode: packs step scratch, checks the totals, prints the budget table, accepts or refuses
execute mode The same DAG with each node calling run. Allocates nothing
replay Execute mode again at the same Dims (a geometry optimization, AIMD), in the same buffers

Three owners

The things one has to decide about a feature belong to three owners. A task answers only for itself; an edge answers for the data between two tasks; the orchestrator derives the rest. Keeping them apart is what stops two tasks from disagreeing about the edge between them, and keeps each task's spec short.

A task declares

  1. Device. CPU or GPU, and the two are different sorts of task. A GPU task holds device rows and pinned rows and runs on a stream, and reaches a pinned row only through a copy it enqueues; a CPU task holds host rows alone, pinned and heap, and runs on the CPU pool, so a CPU task holds no device row and no task holds all three kinds.
  2. Layout. Every buffer it will hold, as a function of its dimensions (nbf, naux, nocc, ...): each one's size, and whether it is scratch, freed when the task ends, or an output that outlives it. The footprint is derived from the layout; nobody writes it. A kernel's share comes from its *_workspace_bytes(dims) query (gpu_layer.md), so the sizes are numbers the kernels own rather than guesses. "Memory: layouts and arenas" below has the whole of it.
  3. Boundary. Its inputs, its outputs, and what it promises not to touch.
  4. Partition, optionally. An axis along which its work can be sliced over workers, and the reduction that recombines the partials. A task that declares none still runs anywhere; it just cannot be split. Most tasks declare none.

A task also obeys three rules, which are what make it the unit of admission, timing, testing and retry:

  • It allocates nothing of its own. Its buffers are carved from an arena the orchestrator planned from its layout, and there is no handle through which a task could allocate anything else. A task asking for a buffer its layout did not name is a contract failure, not a surprise at runtime.
  • It has no internal host sync. It enqueues work on the stream it was given and returns. If part of a computation must wait for another part, that wait is an edge, and the computation is two tasks.
  • Retry means re-admission. A failed GPU allocation aborts in this tree. The place a task can be refused and tried again later is admission, before anything is launched, and nowhere else.

An edge declares

  1. Size. Its bytes, as a formula in the same dimensions. An edge is a known number of bytes, which is the one requirement for it to cross any boundary: a pointer within a GPU, a copy across cards, a transfer across nodes.
  2. Kind. Broadcast (one producer, every consumer reads), reduce (every producer contributes, one consumer reads the combination), gather, or point-to-point.
  3. Reduction order, for a reduce. Ordered, with a fixed combination order and so a bitwise-reproducible result at any worker count, or unordered, with a declared tolerance. The same J task reduced over one slice, four streams or sixteen GPUs gives three results if the reduce is unordered, and the acceptance rule in this tree fixes its bars by analysis.
  4. Lifetime. An edge's buffer lives from its producer's start to its last consumer's end. With the tasks' layouts, that is everything the arena needs to lay a DAG out.

An edge never says which level of the worker hierarchy it crosses. That is placement, and placement is the orchestrator's.

The orchestrator derives

  • Fork and sync points, from the DAG's shape. A node with several successors is a fork; a node with several predecessors is a join.
  • Concurrency, from the shape too. Two nodes with no path between them may run at once, as may a transfer and the compute it does not feed.
  • Placement: which worker each node, or each slice of a partitioned node, lands on.
  • Admission: a schedule, and an arena laid out over every layout and edge lifetime under that schedule, that fits each worker's budget.
  • Transport: what an edge costs given where its two ends landed, and the mechanism that moves it.
  • Error propagation: a failed node fails its successors and nothing else.

In the crates the orchestrator has two halves: the planner, fb_core_planner, which lays a Dag out and places it as a Plan (Dag::plan), and the executor, fb_core_thread_pool, which runs the TaskGraph that plan's Instance builds. "The planner" names fb_core_planner alone.

A builder adds tasks

A computation is not one task, and the code that wires its tasks together is a builder: a public function that takes the DAG and adds nodes to it through Dag::add_gpu and Dag::add_cpu ("Memory: layouts and arenas" below). Every builder has one shape, add_x(dag, worker, inputs…) -> outputs:

  • Named add_*, for what it adds: add_overlap, add_scf.
  • dag: &mut Dag first, after self for a method (Diis::add_push), and worker, when it takes one, second. Then the rows of earlier builders it reads, and the planning input (a basis, a molecule, a step's settings).
  • Rows out: the row ids the next builder reads, which is how one wires to the next. A builder that can refuse its input returns a Result and refuses at planning, before it adds anything, so a refused plan holds no half of a builder.

add_core_hamiltonian is the pattern whole: it refuses nuclei with no nucleus, adds one GPU task, hands that task's row to add_cartesian_to_spherical, and returns the row that adds.

It is a convention a source scan holds, not a trait. A trait could not stop a free function taking a &mut Dag beside it, since Dag::add_gpu is public to every task crate, and nothing yet calls builders generically; each call names its builder. The old binary's table of which tables each calculation required and accepted (deleted in #1873) was not that caller: it checked keys, and each calculation's plan named its builders. A Step trait for the pipeline's steps, never for a recipe's or an integral's builders, is revisited when the first nested chain rerun appears (an optimization, a semi-numerical Hessian or an IRC replanning the chain up to [scf] at each geometry), or when two calculations' plans first build the same product (both calling add_df_eri), whichever comes first. A private function that adds nodes for a builder, and the planner's own Dag methods in core/, are not held to the shape.

Memory: layouts and arenas

The C++ tree's footprints (src/core/footprint, src/pipeline/footprint) were hard to write, and the reason is worth naming, because the model is built to remove it. A footprint there is a second program about allocation: the routine allocates, and beside it a hand-written formula describes what it allocated, held in step with it only by tests against the measured pool peak. Every difficulty follows from that. Scratch lifetimes are implicit in where the code happens to free, so the then / drop algebra reconstructs them. The pool is dynamic, so a fragmentation headroom is left. Adaptive widths are chosen at run time from free memory, so the formula can only state the least. Nested pipelines fold inside outer steps. The shared sizes struct that keeps a routine and its footprint agreeing is a suggestion, not a contract.

This section and the three after it are the principles. The level below, the table's rows and their owners, the lifetime order, the admission arithmetic, the adaptive-width routine and the two blocks' raw calls, is memory.md.

The rebuild makes allocation and footprint one program, in three moves.

A task is two functions. layout(dims) returns the buffers the task will hold: each with a name, a size in bytes and a lifetime (scratch or output), the kernel workspace queries and the solver's lwork queries called here, at planning, as the pipeline already treats syevd_lwork as a dimension, and a BLAS handle's workspace among them (gpu_layer.md, "Layering"). run(bufs, stream) computes, and reaches a buffer only through the layout it declared. The footprint is read off the layout by the orchestrator and never written by the author. The layout is what admission reads, what a tier-3 test checks with no device, and what the arena is built from. In the crate the two are core::planner's GpuTask and CpuTask, one trait per sort: the task's value is its dimensions, so dims is &self, and layout declares through one Layout per memory kind the sort holds, reading an earlier task's output by the row id that task's layout returned. A GPU task's layout is handed a device and a pinned Layout, and its run its device rows as a Bufs, its pinned rows as a CopyOnly that only its stream's copies can take, and the stream. A CPU task's layout is handed a pinned and a heap Layout, host kinds alone, and its run those rows as two Bufs; a loop's Decision is handed the same two. The sort decides the worker: Dag::add_gpu puts a task on its GPU worker's stream, Dag::add_cpu on the CPU pool.

Allocation is a capability, held by one module. The raw allocation bindings in wwr-sys are unsafe, one arena module wraps them, and a task's run receives its buffers already carved. There is no handle a task could allocate through, so "a task allocates nothing of its own" is a compile error rather than a convention. The contract tier's check becomes a debug_requires on the arena: a task may ask only for a buffer its layout named.

The orchestrator lays an arena out at planning. Every buffer's size and lifetime is known once the schedule is chosen, so assigning offsets in one block per worker is an interval-overlap problem solved once, the memory planning a tensor compiler does. What that replaces, from the C++:

C++ today Under an arena
then / drop and the peak model The arena's size. The peak is not estimated; it is the plan.
Footprint tests against the measured pool peak Nothing to measure. The run performs no allocation, so measured equals declared by construction.
The 64 MiB fragmentation headroom None. There is no dynamic pool to fragment.
An adaptive routine's width chosen at run time from free memory Chosen at planning: layout takes the room the schedule leaves and returns the width. Deterministic, and known before the first run.
A nested pipeline's footprint folded into the outer step's A sub-DAG's arena is carved from its parent's. Outputs that outlive a task live in the parent's layout.
Footprint<Device> and Footprint<Pinned> One arena per memory kind, and the kind stays in the type.

Plan-once-run-many is what makes this physical: one arena per planned DAG, carved once, reused every run. An iteration, a geometry or a displacement allocates nothing.

Where offsets may be reused. Two buffers may share an offset only when the schedule guarantees their lifetimes do not overlap. On one stream that is the stream's order. Across streams it is a path in the DAG from the last use of one to the first use of the other, so offset reuse is a scheduling decision, which is fine because the planner owns both.

Sizes the dimensions do not fix. A screened shell-pair count, a Cholesky rank or a Davidson subspace's final size is data-dependent and unknown at planning. There are two honest answers, and the model allows both: a bound from the dimensions, which is the pipeline's answer today (worst-case ncas, the Davidson's largest subspace), and a host sizing node that fixes a sub-DAG's dimensions before that sub-DAG is planned. The bound is preferred; the sizing node is for the case where the bound is too loose to admit.

The arena is a table and a block

Two pieces, and the first is the one with the content.

The offset assigner is a pure planner function. Its input is every buffer in the planned DAG with its size, its alignment and its lifetime, an interval of the schedule on one stream or a DAG-path relation across streams. Its output is an offset per buffer and the block's total. Greedy best-fit placement, the static memory planning a tensor compiler does, run in a few orders and kept at the smallest total (memory.md, "Lifetimes"), is enough for the DAGs this tree has; the general problem is hard, and "The schedule search" under the open questions says when greedy may not be. It touches no device, so it is tier 3 (testing.md, "Tiers: where a check lives"), it is fuzzed as a pure function (testing.md, "Fuzzing"), and its total is the committed golden (testing.md, "Regression").

The block is one allocation plus that table. One contiguous allocation per GPU worker per memory kind, made when the plan is instantiated and freed when the plan drops, and a buffer(id) accessor that returns a slice of the length the layout declared, under the debug contract that the id is in the calling task's layout. It is the one module that calls the raw allocation bindings ("Capabilities are enforced by lints"), and the call is the plain device allocation, not the stream-ordered pool the crate also wraps: one allocation per plan has nothing for a pool to amortise, and the crate's README records hipMallocFromPoolAsync deadlocking beside other threads' stream work on ROCm 7.2.4, a hazard a plain allocation made before any stream work never meets. It is not an allocator. There is no allocate or free at run time, so there are no size classes, no free lists, no coalescing and no compaction, and nothing for a pool library to speed up. A pool is the right tool when sizes arrive in an order nobody knows; here every size and lifetime is known before the first run, and the headroom and the fragmentation a pool carries are what the table above removes.

Blocks, plans and the process

Three lifetimes, each owning the next.

What it is How many Lives
Process The running binary: one config, one logger, the devices (num_gpus of them, "The run's devices"), the worker budget One main to exit
Plan A DAG with its dimensions fixed, scheduled, placed and admitted: the offset tables, the worker assignment, the sync points One per calculation; the pipeline's, with its steps' and their iterations' nested inside Planning to drop, run any number of times between
Block One contiguous allocation of one memory kind on one GPU worker, sized to the plan's peak for that worker and kind Per top-level plan, one per GPU worker it uses, per kind The plan's instantiation to its drop

A run is a plan being executed. It owns nothing: it borrows the plan's blocks and workers for its duration and allocates nothing. The process owns the devices, the budgets the planner admits against and the threads the orchestrator opens around a run, and no memory of its own. There is no process-wide pool, because a process block could be sized only two ways: by knowing every plan it will serve before allocating, which is a plan of plans, and that is the pipeline plan; or by allocating a guess and growing when a plan does not fit, which is a pool, with the reallocation and the headroom the arena exists to remove. The config fixes the whole calculation up front, so the first way is available and is the model as written.

A sub-DAG has a table but no block. Its offsets are laid out inside its parent's block, in the region the parent's schedule leaves free while the sub-DAG runs, and the outputs that outlive it are in the parent's table. Only top-level plans allocate. The semi-numerical Hessian's pinned block is the top-level plan's, sized to one gradient sub-DAG's peak once, not 6 natoms times.

The peak is a maximum over the schedule, not a sum. A block equals its plan's peak, the largest total of simultaneously live buffers under the schedule, and every run reaches it at some instant, so no design runs the plan in less. The SCF step's scratch and the gradient step's alias in time inside the pipeline plan because their lifetimes are disjoint, which is the process-wide sharing a pool would do by accident, done on purpose. Two top-level plans alive at once hold their sum; the answer is to make them one plan, and nesting is how. A plan kept alive after its last run holds its block while something else runs, and the answer there is that plans are values: drop the finished one.

Replay allocates nothing. The coordinates are an edge, not a dimension. A geometry optimization, an IRC walk or a molecular-dynamics trajectory is one plan run once per geometry, with the same offsets and the same pages, and its two allocations are at the start and the end of the calculation. What varies with geometry, a screened shell-pair count or a Cholesky rank, takes the bound from the dimensions ("Sizes the dimensions do not fix"), so memory sits idle at most geometries in exchange for a trajectory that never calls the driver, which is the right trade inside a loop. Even a sizing node that re-plans a sub-DAG per run allocates nothing: the parent reserves the sub-DAG's region at its bound, and re-planning recomputes a table inside it.

Concurrency is a width the planner admits. Two sub-DAGs in flight cannot alias, so each one adds its peak to the parent's block. How many of the independent sub-DAGs run at once, the displacements, the fragments, the roots, is the smaller of what memory admits and what the workers allow, from one up, and both numbers are the plan's. Serializing is how a plan fits; parallelism is the room the chosen order leaves, as the first section says. The reduce at the join combines the partials in the declared order whatever the width, so the width is a planning decision and not a correctness one.

Pinned memory is per GPU. Physically it is the node's, page-locked host RAM any card can read, and the C ABI's portable flag is set on both backends so that fact does not depend on the unified address space. But every pinned buffer exists to feed one GPU's copy engines, and its lifetime is bounded by a copy on that GPU's stream, so its owner is that GPU worker, as the C++ tree's DeviceResources already has it. A GPU worker is then one self-contained unit, its streams, its device block and its pinned block, admitted together against the per-GPU caps, and NUMA locality comes free from allocating the block on the worker's own thread. The node is a cap and not an owner: the sum of a node's pinned blocks and the plan's heap block must fit the host's available memory, which is the C++ check_pinned_node check carried over, with the heap beside it. Host data broadcast to several cards is placed in one consumer's pinned block and read across, which the portable flag makes legal and the "GPUs on one node" row of the worker table prices, or staged once per card; both are placement. Pinning costs milliseconds and locks pages, so once per plan, reused every run, is where plan-once-run-many pays most.

Pinned is staging; large flat host data is a heap row. A pinned row exists to feed or drain a copy on a stream, and page-locked memory spent on anything else is locked for no reason: the SCF decision's carried energy never meets a copy engine, and host data too large to lock, B kept on the host past the pinned carve, has to live somewhere counted. Such data is a heap row: a slice of an Element, sized at planning, in the plan's one heap block, which the assigner lays out as it does any table, so its footprint is the table and there is no second formula. Only a CPU node holds one; when it must reach the device, a pinned row is the window it streams through. The heap block is one global-allocator call at instantiation, freed at the plan's drop: an arena, not an allocator, so a run allocates nothing on the host either.

Structured planning input is Vec. The config, a BasisSet and the planner's own tables exist before any table can be sized, and fix its dimensions, so they cannot be rows: they are Vec, not modelled and not admitted. Making an arena the backend for Vec means the unstable allocator API or a third-party Vec across every host type, for no property the model asks for. What a run hands its caller after it, a Vec copied out of its last row, is the one copy a run makes (memory.md, "The three kinds").

What no arena reaches. A vendor routine that allocates internally, as some solver paths do whatever workspace they are handed, is outside every arena. The answer is the one the C++ tree uses, the lwork and *_workspace_bytes queries so the routine has no reason to allocate, and the memory golden is what notices when one does anyway.

Plan once, run many

The replayed computations in this tree share one property: their dimensions do not change between runs. An SCF iteration, the gradient at each geometry of an optimization or an IRC walk, and each displacement of a semi-numerical Hessian all reuse the same basis, so the same nbf and naux. The model makes that a first-class split:

  • Planning fixes the dimensions, evaluates every footprint and edge size, chooses a placement and a schedule, and admits the whole against the budget. It happens once.
  • Running moves data through the planned DAG. It happens any number of times, and nothing is re-admitted.

One run at a time, enforced by types. Instance::run takes the instance and the backend by &mut, as fb_core_thread_pool::run takes the plan and the backend, so neither is ever in two runs at once. The run model needs exactly that: a block's row borrow flags are host-side and a GPU task's rows are returned while its device work may still be in flight, and a Device backend's events are one plan's nodes' and its streams and library handles one run's workers'. The concurrency the model does have keeps working. Within a run the workers share the instance's plan and the backend by reference, through a wrapper only run can make (a Backend is sealed and need not be Sync, and a Device is not, so outside a run no backend reaches a second thread at all); independent (instance, backend) pairs run on threads of their own; and several GPUs are one run, a Device spanning its GPU workers' devices.

Stream capture into a replayed graph is the same split one level down, which is a sign the split is real rather than imposed. A loop is therefore not a special node: it is a planned sub-DAG run repeatedly, with a host decision between runs. The decision is a node (convergence, a step accepted, the walk reaching a minimum) whose output is read on the host.

The executor replays the body in the run it is in. In fb_core_thread_pool, a loop is its body, the nodes added after a given one, and a decision node TaskGraph::add_loop adds after the body's sinks and any node before the body it is given, so after every body node. The decision is a CPU node, so a worker has waited on the host for the body's sinks before it runs, and every GPU body node's work precedes theirs on the device, so it waits on no other: the body's work has completed, and its rows and events are free to be used again, when the decision returns. Its Continue readies the body again, under the run's one lock, in the same scope and on the same workers, so a replay spawns no thread and allocates nothing; its Break readies what follows the loop, which depends on the decision and never on a body node. The decision is handed the iteration's index. The iteration count has a bound, fixed at planning beside the dimensions: a decision that would start one iteration past it fails the run with MaxIterationsExceeded, and a failed body node ends the loop and skips what follows it, as any failure skips its descendants.

The planner closes a loop the same way. fb_core_planner::Dag::add_loop takes the position of the body's first task, its bound and a Decision: a CPU node that declares host rows as a task does, reads what the body copied to the host for it, and returns Continue or Break. The body's rows are a table laid out once, in a region of each worker's tables the parent holds while the loop runs, and the rows it carries between iterations are the parent's (memory.md, "Nested plans"), so a replay is admitted with the plan and allocates nothing.

A counted loop has a trip count and a depth, and no decision. Beside the decision loop, whose next iteration waits on a host answer, a loop over the batches of a streamed tensor knows how many iterations it has at planning, and a host decision per batch would sync the host once a batch and keep a batch's upload from overlapping the GEMMs of the one before. TaskGraph::add_counted and Dag::add_counted close the body over a trip count N and a depth R, both fixed at planning beside the dimensions:

  • Ordering. Iteration k waits on iteration k - R alone: each body node with no predecessor in the body runs after each one with no successor there, R iterations back. That is the one edge across iterations, the ring. Each body node also runs after itself in the iteration before, which on a lane is the stream's order anyway and on the CPU pool keeps a row the body accumulates into in order. Within an iteration the body's edges hold as in any plan.
  • No host wait. Every edge is satisfied as any edge is, so between lanes it is a wait on the device and the GPU worker's thread enqueues every iteration back to back; the run's record holds no sync point for a GPU body node. A CPU body node waits on the host for what it reads, off the device's path.
  • The iteration index. A body node is handed its iteration's index, k from 0, by which it picks its batch: the executor's Lane, and a task's views (Bufs::iteration).
  • Memory. The body's rows are R copies of its table, iteration k in copy k mod R, and a row it accumulates into across iterations is the parent's (memory.md, "Nested plans").
  • Events. Each GPU body node has an event pair per iteration, so each iteration's stream scope is its own, read after the run, and no wait in the run finds its event recorded again by a later iteration.

For batched density fitting the body is an upload on a copy lane and the GEMMs on a compute lane, with R = 2: the next batch's upload runs under this one's GEMMs, ordered against the GEMMs two batches back by the ring. A node after a counted loop may follow a body node directly, and runs after its last iteration.

A counted loop may lie in a decision loop's body. J and K over a streamed B run every SCF iteration, so the SCF's body holds a counted loop over B's batches; that is the one nesting built. A counted loop holds no loop, and a decision loop no decision loop. The counted loop is closed before the decision, and the executor unrolls its trips inside the decision loop's run of nodes, so the decision's Continue readies it whole: every trip again, overlapped as at the top level, with no host wait between trips. The decision runs after the body's sinks, which every trip precedes, and before its replay reads the stream scope of each trip of each GPU body node, its events about to be recorded again. A body node of the counted loop is handed its trip; the decision loop's iteration is the timing record's (diagnostics.md, "The timing record"). An edge leaving only the counted loop, to a later node of the decision loop's body, runs from its last trip; one leaving the decision loop runs from the decision. The counted loop's region is a row of the decision loop's body table (memory.md, "Nested plans").

A wait on an event binds to its recording at the time the wait is enqueued, on both backends: record an event, enqueue a wait for it on another stream, record it again, and the wait is released by the first recording. The host enqueues ahead of the device, so a design that waits on an event while later recordings of it are being enqueued rests on this; the counted loop does not today, since each iteration has events of its own, and a ring of R events per node, which would, is open to it.

DAGs nest. A node may be a sub-DAG with dimensions of its own. The pipeline is a DAG of steps; a step is a DAG of tasks; the RHF step's body is a sub-DAG replayed per iteration. Dimensions attach to the sub-DAG, not to the whole plan: fragments of a many-body expansion each have their own nbf, and the pipeline's one global dimensions struct is the one-calculation special case.

Two kinds of distribution

The topologies we want are a single GPU, a node of GPUs, and many nodes of GPUs. A large coupled-cluster calculation would spread one task's work over every GPU it can reach; a many-body expansion would spread independent fragments over GPUs and nodes. These are two different kinds of distribution, and only one of them costs a task's author anything.

  • Across sub-DAGs. Fragments of an expansion, displacements of a semi-numerical Hessian, the roots of a state average: independent sub-DAGs, joined by a small reduce at the end (a sum of fragment energies, a Hessian assembled from gradients). The orchestrator gets this for free by placement. Nothing inside the tasks changes. How many run at once is the width the planner admits ("Blocks, plans and the process").
  • Within a task. The amplitudes of a coupled-cluster iteration, the Coulomb build over slices of the shell-pair list: one task's work sliced over workers, a broadcast in and a reduce out. This is the partition a task may declare, and it is the only place distribution reaches into a task. It is built as a task per worker over its slice, Partition's range, with Dag::broadcast in and Dag::gather or Dag::reduce out ("Collectives"); the slice is the task's dimension, so the task is still written once, for one stream.

Keeping the partition opt-in is what keeps single-GPU code honest: a task without one is written once, for one stream, and is still placeable on any worker of any topology.

The single-GPU case is the degenerate one: broadcast is a pointer, reduce is an in-place accumulate, and the workers are streams. One DAG description serves every row, which is the point of wanting all of them.

Between the GPUs of one node the transport is the vendor's collectives library, NCCL on CUDA and RCCL on HIP, through WarpWraps' C ABI and its safe wrapper, fb_core_wwr::ccl. A run is one process holding all its devices, so its communicators are one clique, made at once over the devices (Comm::init_all), a rank per device, and each rank driven by the thread of the GPU worker on its device. A broadcast, a reduce, a gather of equal pieces and a point-to-point send are each one call per rank, enqueued on that rank's stream like any launch, so the row's bytes move on the devices with no host copy and no host wait; the library picks NVLink, PCIe peer access or a host bounce from the topology. The row across nodes is still open ("Open questions").

A GPU worker is one card, one thread, one block per kind, and several streams, its lanes. A GPU node is placed on a lane of its worker (fb_core_thread_pool::LaneId), and the worker's tables and blocks are its lanes', so the "streams on one GPU" row costs what it says: the reader reads the producer's row in place. How an edge is ordered follows from where its two ends landed:

Edge Ordering
Same lane The stream's order; no event
Different lanes of one worker An event the producer's lane records and the consumer's waits on, on the device; no host wait
Lane to CPU A host wait on the producer's event, a sync point

The worker's one thread enqueues its ready nodes in rank order across all its lanes and never waits on the device, so lanes overlap on the card as streams do, and the thread count stays one per worker however many lanes it has. The C++ src/integrals/mmd is the pattern adapted: a stream there belongs to a piece of data, each primitive-pair class carrying its own, its readers enqueued on it, and one host thread feeds every stream, since an enqueue does not block. Here too the code that builds the DAG names the lane, as mmd names one per class; deriving lanes from the DAG's shape is not built ("Open questions"). A worker's lanes are the ones its tasks name, so the count grows as the builder names them and is fixed when the DAG is planned, before the backend makes one stream per lane.

Each lane has its own library handles. Two lanes run at once on the card, and a BLAS or solver handle's state and workspace are one stream's at a time, so the Device backend makes a BLAS and a dense-solver handle per lane, bound to its stream (gpu_layer.md, "Layering"), and a declared BLAS workspace is a worker row per lane. A lane above 0 whose tasks all say they call no library (GpuTask::uses_libraries), as the mmd records' classes and the copy nodes do, has neither.

Reuse across lanes is a handoff, and every one serializes. The assigner packs a worker's table to the fewest bytes against the plan's ranks, whatever lane a node is on, and every reuse it makes is a handoff the planner adds as an edge (memory.md, "Lifetimes"). Between two lanes that edge is an event, so a row that takes another lane's bytes waits for that lane's last user, and two lanes whose rows share bytes do not overlap there. The rule is the one the C++ mmd makes by hand, whose classes' sorts share one carve, "which is why the forks chain": bytes win over concurrency, always, and the planner adds the event edge uniformly, through the handoffs, rather than exploiting only the paths the DAG already has. Giving a reuse up for concurrency when the cap leaves room is admission's search, and not built (memory.md, "Open questions").

The run's devices

A run holds num_gpus devices, 0..num_gpus. The config's top-level num_gpus (the old binary's Config::num_gpus, default 1) is how many devices the process holds for the run, devices 0 to num_gpus - 1 in that order, its device list. 0 is refused as the file is read, before any device is touched, and a count above the devices the runtime sees is refused, naming both numbers, before the plan is made; at 1 the runtime is not asked, so a config without the key runs as it did before the key existed. GPU worker w is on the list's device w.

Work spreads where a builder names the workers. Placement is the builder's: it names each task's worker, so a builder that spreads names workers 0..num_gpus, one per device, and one that does not puts every task on worker 0. The instance makes each worker's blocks on its device and the backend its lanes' streams, handles and fork streams there (Plan::instantiate(&devices), Instance::backend), for the plan's workers alone, and the clique of communicators when the plan holds a collective ("Collectives", below); a device past the plan's last worker holds nothing. So a plan whose tasks are all on worker 0 is, at any num_gpus, the plan of one device: its plan.txt, plan.dot and products are unchanged by the key. Two workers on one device are allowed, and run as two threads with blocks of their own, but not with a collective, whose clique needs a device per rank.

What crosses between workers. A task reads only rows of its own worker's tables, which are on its device. A row reaches another worker by a collective ("Collectives"); an ordering reaches one through a CPU node, which waits on the host for the producer's event, or, in the executor, through an edge between lanes of two workers, a wait on the consumer's device for an event recorded on the producer's. Both runtimes let a stream wait on an event of another device in one process, so that edge costs no host wait, as between two lanes of one worker. The planner adds no such edge of its own yet; the collectives need none.

What an edge across devices costs, the "GPUs on one node" row, is not priced. The planner chooses no placement, so it has no price to compare; the builder that names the workers is choosing with the cost in mind, and the plan's text names every collective's parts, and its tables the rows each moves, which is what a price would read ("Open questions").

One lane is privileged: lane 0 of the worker on the list's first device. Its DeviceStream says so (DeviceStream::is_privileged), set when the Device backend makes the run's streams and false on every other lane, and on a stream made outside a backend (DeviceStream::new), which is no run's. It is the lane that owns the run's run-wide work, the host I/O and the reductions every device's partials meet in: a builder that spreads gathers and reduces onto worker 0, and every collective's part there runs on that lane. On one device it is the lane every one-lane plan already runs on. Nothing reads the flag yet.

Collectives

A collective is one step of the plan with a part on every GPU worker. Dag::add_collective(name, parts) adds part w as a GPU task on lane 0 of worker w, at consecutive positions, each part's layout holding its own worker's rows; the parts exchange the rows on the devices, each part calling its rank of the clique (DeviceStream::comm) on its lane's stream. No edge joins the parts: the collective completes on each device when every rank has issued its part, which the library waits for on the devices, not on the host. The builders over it are the planner's, as the copy nodes are:

Builder Moves Result
Dag::broadcast a root's row to every worker each worker's row, the root's own the source; exact
Dag::gather each worker's piece, rows split.range(w) of a Partition, onto a root one row of split.total() there; exact, the pieces disjoint
Dag::reduce each worker's partial, summed onto a root one row there; unordered, the library's order

Every worker issues the collectives in plan order. A rank whose part of one collective is enqueued after its part of the next leaves both waiting on the devices for good, and the executor enqueues a worker's ready nodes in rank order but only the ready ones. So the plan orders each part after its worker's part of the collective before, or after the decision of the loop that held that one; the parts are on lane 0, one stream per worker, so the device keeps the order the thread issued. Every other wait a part can meet is on an event already recorded or a CPU node that waits on parts already issued, so no cycle forms.

The clique is every worker's, made with the backend. A collective spans every GPU worker of its plan (a contract at Dag::plan, which counts the ranks, Plan::ranks), and the backend of a plan that holds one makes the clique over its workers' devices, a rank per worker, before any run (Device::make_clique), its ranks dropped with the backend. A plan with none makes none, so the binary's plans pay nothing for it.

Where a collective may lie. In a decision loop's body whole or not at all, the next iteration's parts issued after the decision, which waits for this iteration's; in no counted loop's, whose trips in flight could issue two collectives on two workers in two orders. Both are contracts of Dag::add_loop and Dag::add_counted.

Forks inside a task

A lane is the planner's: nodes are placed on it, the executor orders the edges between lanes, and it costs a stream, library handles and a workspace row. Some concurrency lives inside one node instead, where the DAG does not look: a build of many small kernels can launch one per primitive-pair class into one matrix, every class adding atomically, as the C++ launch_each does on a stream per class. A GPU task reaches it through its stream, DeviceStream::fork_join(n, |i, fork| ...):

  • Fork. The task's stream records an event, and each fork stream waits on it, so each starts after everything already on the task's stream: the executor's waits for its inputs and what the task enqueued before the call.
  • Items. The closure runs once per item, on the host and in order, each enqueueing on its fork; item i goes on fork i mod k. The items are not ordered among themselves, so they must not conflict: they add atomically, as the class kernels do, or write disjoint bytes.
  • Join. Each fork records an event, and the task's stream waits on each, before fork_join returns. Every wait is on the device; a fork is no host sync, and a task that forks is still one task ("Granularity").

The forks are a worker's own streams, not its lanes. Each GPU worker has a pool of fork streams beside its lanes, with no library handles, made with the backend as its lanes are, never during a run. Its size is the most any of the worker's tasks declares it forks onto (GpuTask::forks, none by default), and k is the smaller of that and n; with fewer than two, the items run on the task's stream itself, so a count left out costs concurrency, never order. Forking onto another lane's stream, as the C++ forks onto a stream each class carries, would order the task behind whatever is queued on that lane, a dependency the planner cannot see. Two nodes of one worker that fork share its pool, so their forked items keep the order the worker's thread enqueued them in; that never deadlocks, since the one thread enqueues all of a worker's work and every wait it enqueues is on an event already recorded. No node is placed on a fork and no edge names one, so the plan's text, its tables and its goldens are those of the same DAG without forks.

The scope is what makes a fork sound. The forks start after the task's input waits and are joined before its body returns, so everything the executor records on the task's lane afterwards covers the forked work: its completion event, which every consumer on another lane waits on, and every handoff of its rows' bytes to a later owner. What a row's DeviceSlice promise rests on, that a launch goes on the stream the executor ordered, holds in effect. The API allows no fork without its join: a fork stream is reached only as the closure's borrowed &Stream, as a scoped thread is only inside std::thread::scope, and a failed item or fork still joins every fork already made before the error returns, as launch_each does. A worker drains its forks with its lanes as it exits, so a run returns with nothing outstanding even after a failed join.

CPU workers

A CPU node is a file write, a host decision between runs of a sub-DAG, or a small host-side assembly. It runs on a CPU worker, which is one thread the orchestrator owns. The needs here are meagre: the products written when a step finishes, an intermediate tensor dumped for debugging, a convergence check. The design is sized to them, and it is enforced rather than hoped for, because the place a thread count gets out of hand is never the one that was designed.

A run owns a fixed set of threads. At the start of a run the orchestrator opens one std::thread::scope and creates n_cpu CPU workers and one thread per GPU worker inside it, whatever its lanes; the scope joins them all when the run ends. A ready queue of node ids feeds the CPU workers, another per GPU worker feeds its thread across all its lanes, and a GPU worker's thread has its device made current for the life of the run (gpu_layer.md, "Layering"). No node is ever given a thread of its own, so the thread count is a property of the plan, fixed at planning, and not of the DAG's size. "Too many threads" is then impossible for the same reason "too much memory" is once the arena is laid out: there is no counter to check because there is no spawn in the loop.

Sub-DAGs borrow their parent's workers. This is where a count would blow up otherwise. The semi-numerical Hessian is 6 natoms gradient sub-DAGs, and if each opened a scope of its own, one config line would make hundreds of threads. The rule is the arena rule restated: a sub-DAG's arena is carved from its parent's, and a sub-DAG's nodes are scheduled onto its parent's workers. Only the top-level run owns threads. Nesting adds nodes to the queue, never workers, and a loop re-adds its body's nodes to it, as the thread-count test below checks across a replay.

The count is a budget, admitted beside memory. n_cpu defaults to the host's available_parallelism less the GPU workers' threads, floored at one, and the config overrides it beside the [memory] caps. A placement that needs more CPU workers than the budget is refused in the same admission pass that refuses an arena that does not fit.

Fan-out is nodes, not a pool inside a task. Writing three files when the RHF step finishes is three CPU nodes with no edge between them, each reading an edge from the step; the orchestrator sees three ready nodes and runs them at once. A task that opened a pool of its own to do the same (the host integrals below are the one exception, and say why) would hide the parallelism from the planner and would log from threads that carry no task record (diagnostics.md, "The task context"). One thread runs one node, and the orchestrator sets the record on that thread before the node starts, so the record needs no forwarding. A debugging dump in the middle of an iteration has the same shape: a device-to-pinned copy node on the stream, then a CPU write node that depends on it and feeds nothing. The next iteration's GPU work has no edge to the write, so it proceeds while the write runs, and the overlap costs the author nothing. The model makes two things explicit that a hand-written dump would leave implicit: the pinned buffer is in the layout, with its lifetime extended to the writer's end, so admission counts it; and under plan-once-run-many a dump switched on is a different plan, not a branch inside a task.

The standard library, and no pool crate. What the orchestrator needs is a queue, a condition variable and the scoped threads, under a hundred lines, most of them the loop that pops a node id and runs it. Shutdown is the scope's join. A release build aborts on a panic, and a test build propagates it through the join (diagnostics.md, "Panics and profiles"), but only if the other workers exit: a worker that panics mid-node never marks it finished, and the rest would wait for it forever. So each worker holds a guard that, while unwinding, marks the run aborted and wakes everyone, and the join re-raises the node's own panic. The work that would justify a dependency is the scheduler, readiness counts and the choice of which ready node goes to which worker, and no crate has it in the DAG-with-placement shape the model wants, so it is written under either choice. The usual crates would each add something unwanted. Rayon is a work-stealing scheduler, and stealing is placement, which is the orchestrator's; its idle workers spin before they sleep; and a GPU worker is a thread dedicated to one device, which a general pool has no notion of. Tokio is for many sockets, and its file I/O is a blocking thread pool underneath. The threadpool crate's 'static bound on a job forces every edge buffer behind an Arc and defeats the scoped borrow. The workers live behind one type in one module, so that if a profile ever asked for stealing on the CPU side a crate could slot in behind the same type.

The host integrals run on rayon, and nothing else does. The one-electron matrices S, T and V and the density-fitting metric (P|Q) are built on the host, not on a card ("The MainBus and its steps", above): each is a loop over shell pairs, one rayon task per center writing a disjoint run of rows of a packed triangle, with nothing in it for the planner to place. They run on rayon's threads, called from inside one step, a second pool beside the orchestrator's workers with a thread count of its own. Stealing there is no placement the plan cares about, for the reason a GPU task's forks are none ("Forks inside a task"): the tasks start and finish inside one node, and each crate's the_thread_count_does_not_change_a_bit holds the triangle bitwise the same on one thread as on eight. The crates do not log, so no line comes from a thread without a task record. The crates that may name rayon are those four, under rust/integrals/: fb-integrals-s-overlap-host, fb-integrals-t-kinetic-host, fb-integrals-v-potential-en-host and fb-integrals-df-metric. A crate above them reaches rayon only through them, never by an edge of its own, and no member depends on any other pool crate but fb-core-flow on rustflow (below). Each needs the FMA target feature, which the workspace's .cargo/config.toml sets for every member, so a dependent built from rust/ gets it. The pool is one HostPool (in fb-integrals-df-metric, since only these crates may name rayon), which fb-steps ("The MainBus and its steps") starts in setup with [resources] host_threads threads: by default the host's available_parallelism less the executor's threads, floored at one, as n_cpu above, and 0 refused. Every host integral call from a step runs inside its install, so rayon's global pool is never built, and the pool's threads are joined when the bus drops. A run's threads are then the executor's and the pool's beside the main thread, which steps/tests/steps_threads.rs counts exactly after a device-free run and again after the plan drops. Each crate writes its triangle into a caller's slice (overlap_into and its kin), so a host step builds it in its pinned staging and its run allocates nothing of its size (memory.md, "The budget").

The v0.0.4 layer's executor, and its fan-out. The step DAG runs on rustflow's Executor ("Steps on rustflow"), not on the orchestrator's workers: one per process, its thread count fixed at init from the config, a work-stealing scheduler whose stealing moves which thread runs a step, never where it runs, since a step's GPUs are fixed before budget mode. rustflow brings crossbeam-deque, the second exception to "no pool crate": fb-core-flow is the one member that names rustflow, and it re-exports Executor, so a crate above it reaches the executor only through it. A sync blocks its worker, so the width covers the GPU steps that can wait at once plus the CPU work, a handful of threads. An all-GPU step's inner work across its GPUs is fb_core_flow::fan_out: one scoped thread per GPU for the length of the fan-out, each making its device current, joined before it returns, run inline for one GPU. It is the one place the layer spawns, and the host integrals' rayon pool, called from a step, is the other pool beside the executor. Outside the layer, the host-only stamp generator tools/sad runs each element's serial atomic SCF on scoped threads of its own in its compute, as many as the CPU affinity allows, joined before it returns: it has no executor, and no pool crate may serve it.

What is tested. Every node runs exactly once, in a topological order, on no more threads than the plan said. The last is a tier-3 test (testing.md, "Tiers: where a check lives") that reads the Threads: line of /proc/self/status after a device-free run and compares it to the plan's worker total plus the main thread, over GPU workers of several lanes each, and reads the same count before a loop's first iteration and after its last. It is the one check that catches what a lint cannot see: a linked host library opening a pool of its own at load, as a host BLAS does. A HIP build links one, ROCm's host OpenBLAS, through hipSOLVER: its load-time constructor starts one thread per core before main, and no call of the tree runs on it. So the v0.0.4 layer's setup, before it starts a thread of its own, sets it to one thread and joins that pool (fb_core_device::single_thread_host_blas, which finds OpenBLAS's controls by name in the loaded objects and does nothing on a build without one), and steps/tests/steps_threads.rs counts against the main thread alone, which is what notices when that slips.

Capabilities are enforced by lints

Three things a task may not do are each held by named code: allocating is the arena's ("Memory: layouts and arenas"), spawning is the worker modules', and unsafe is a listed few crates'. Each is a lint, in the manifest's [lints] table that documentation.md ("Code: rustdoc") already uses for missing_docs or at a crate's root, so a violation is a build failure in every invocation, the gates and an editor alike, and not a convention a review has to remember.

Spawning. std::thread cannot be removed from the standard library, but clippy's disallowed_methods makes calling it a compile error everywhere but the modules with the allow:

# clippy.toml
disallowed-methods = [
  { path = "std::thread::spawn", reason = "workers belong to the orchestrator, or to an all-GPU step's fan-out" },
  { path = "std::thread::scope", reason = "workers belong to the orchestrator, or to an all-GPU step's fan-out" },
  { path = "std::thread::Builder::spawn", reason = "workers belong to the orchestrator, or to an all-GPU step's fan-out" },
  { path = "std::thread::Builder::spawn_scoped", reason = "workers belong to the orchestrator, or to an all-GPU step's fan-out" },
]

The allows are two modules: core::thread_pool's run, which opens the old layer's workers, and core::flow's fan_out, an all-GPU step's one scoped thread per GPU ("CPU workers"). rustflow's executor spawns its own workers, out of clippy's sight, which is why the manifest holds it. The manifest does the rest: rayon is a dependency of the four host integral crates alone, rustflow of fb-core-flow alone ("CPU workers"), tokio and every other pool crate of none, and a pool crate arriving later is a Cargo.toml diff a review sees.

unsafe. The rebuild's unsafe is the FFI into wwr-sys and the kernel libraries, and the code that wraps it. unsafe_code is rustc's own lint, and forbid is the level that cannot be allowed back, so it is set per crate: a crate on the list below leaves it at its default, and every other crate forbids it with #![forbid(unsafe_code)] at its root. That is an attribute rather than a manifest line because Cargo refuses a member that inherits [workspace.lints] and adds a [lints.rust] table of its own. The list is in two tiers:

  • Core: fb-core-wwr, fb-core-arena, fb-core-log, fb-core-thread-pool, fb-core-device, fb-core-budget.
  • Bindings: fb-integrals-sys, fb-localization-sys, fb-recipes-sys.

The core crates hold the wrappers: fb-core-wwr over the wwr-sys bindings (and the statvfs of /dev/shm a collectives' system error reports), the arena over the raw allocation and copy bindings, the two libc calls in core::log, and the one unsafe impl Sync in core::thread_pool, on run's private wrapper of the backend it shares with its workers, which rests on a run borrowing the backend exclusively and on Backend being sealed ("Plan once, run many"). fb-core-wwr also holds the unsafe impl Send of the handles a step carries between workers: Stream and Event, Sync as well, on the runtime's thread safety; the BLAS and dense-solver handles, Send but never Sync, since the libraries allow a handle on any thread one call at a time. Each impl states its argument and is asserted at compile time beside it. The CCL rank stays neither. The binding crates declare a kernel library's extern "C" functions, check its version at load, and wrap its launches safely beside the bindings, over fb-core-device's stream and slices, each call uploading the indices its kernel trusts itself; the recipes' safe calls are fb-core-device's, which calls fb-recipes-sys's bindings itself (gpu_layer.md, "The v0.0.4 runtime"). There was a third tier, the method crates, whose unsafe was a direct call into their kernels' launchers, the BLAS or the solver; each such call has moved into a safe wrapper in the core and binding crates, and a method crate that needs a new one adds it there rather than joining the list. fb-doc-check's tests/unsafe_code.rs reads this list: a crate root that neither forbids unsafe_code nor is listed fails it, and so does a listed crate with no unsafe left, so the list only shrinks.

Where unsafe is allowed, clippy's undocumented_unsafe_blocks requires a // SAFETY: comment on every block, which turns the comment the crate already writes into a rule and names the blocks that lack one, and multiple_unsafe_ops_per_block keeps each block to one operation so the comment justifies exactly one thing. Miri covers what a lint cannot (testing.md, "Sanitizers").

A library call reaches the crates above core through a safe wrapper, not wwr-sys: fb_core_wwr::blas::gemm and dgmm and fb_core_wwr::solver::syevd take their operands as DeviceSlices (device memory with its length, which the arena's rows implement) and check each extent against them in every build (syevd's work against the solver's own query), and fb_core_wwr::memset::zero zeroes one on a stream. A kernel library's launch is wrapped the same way in its binding crate, beside the generated bindings: fb-integrals-sys's mmd_records, three_center_spherical_class and packed_gather take fb-core-device's slices, assert every length, check every index their kernel follows on the host and upload it themselves, and map the launch status through fb_kernel_abi::launch_status; the recipes' launches are fb_core_device::GpuResources' methods, on the same terms. That is the shape for the rest. The BLAS workspace, which the library keeps beyond any borrow, is handed over by value instead: BlasHandle::bind_workspace owns an arena HeldRow until the handle is destroyed or rebound (gpu_layer.md, "Layering"). Every crate above the core and binding crates now has nothing left to call raw and forbids unsafe: fb-steps and the four host integral crates among them.

disallowed_methods = "deny"
undocumented_unsafe_blocks = "deny"
multiple_unsafe_ops_per_block = "deny"
missing_panics_doc = "deny"

Reproducibility across placements

The same planned DAG must give the same answer, to a declared tolerance, under every placement. Where every reduce is ordered, that tolerance is zero and the result is bitwise. Where a reduce is unordered, its declared tolerance is the bar, and the test tier compares the one-worker result against a many-worker one at that bar (testing.md, "Tiers: where a check lives", tiers 4 and 5). Tolerances are fixed by analysis and never loosened to make a comparison pass.

Example: the Coulomb build under two placements

The DAG, declared once:

  • Task J: GPU. Layout per slice: the partial J (nbf², an output) and the integral scratch, sized by the kernel's workspace query. Boundary: reads D, writes its partial, touches nothing else. Partition: the shell-pair list, recombined by an ordered sum.
  • Edge D: nbf² bytes, broadcast. Freed after the last slice, and after K, which reads it too.
  • Edge J: nbf² bytes per slice, reduce, ordered.
  • Derived: the fork is the partition; the join is the reduce; K is a sibling of J, not a successor, because it shares D and nothing else.

The same declarations under two placements:

One GPU Three GPUs
Workers Three streams, or one if admission leaves no room for three partials One card per slice
Broadcast D's pointer D copied to each card
Reduce Each slice accumulates into J in slice order Partials copied to one card, summed in slice order
Concurrency K on a fourth stream if its footprint fits beside J's K on any card with room

Neither the task nor the edges changed. Only the orchestrator's answers did.

A second example costs one sentence: the semi-numerical Hessian is 6 natoms gradient sub-DAGs, each the whole gradient DAG with the same dimensions, independent until the final assembly. On one GPU they run in sequence; on a node they spread across cards; the gradient DAG is declared once.

Granularity

A task is the smallest unit with its own footprint and no internal host sync. That rules out both ends:

  • A kernel launch is below the line. A launch wrapper allocates nothing and syncs nothing, so it has no footprint to admit and nothing to time but the stream.
  • A pipeline step is above the line. It allocates many things with different lifetimes and syncs between them, so it is a sub-DAG, and its syncs are its edges.

The practical test is the second rule: if a computation must wait for part of itself, split it there.

Open questions

  • Where the line is in practice. A sync that is free on one GPU, two kernels on one stream, still splits a task under this rule. Is that the right cost, or should a task be allowed a chain of launches on one stream with no edge between them? The current answer is that a chain on one stream is not a sync, so it stays one task; the rule is about host waits.
  • Adaptive layouts and the schedule. An adaptive task's width depends on the room the schedule leaves, and the schedule's peak depends on the widths. Is one pass enough, laying the fixed layouts out first and handing each adaptive task the remainder in schedule order, as check_plan's room line does today, or does the planner iterate?
  • Lanes from the DAG's shape. The builder names each GPU node's lane today, as the C++ mmd names a stream per class. Two nodes with no path between them could be put on two lanes by the planner instead, but a lane is a stream, and a library handle pair and a BLAS workspace row when its tasks call a library, so the count is a cost, and which nodes are worth splitting is the schedule search's question below. Forks inside a task do not answer it: they are concurrency within one node, which the DAG does not see into ("Forks inside a task").
  • How loose is too loose. A bound from the dimensions is the preferred answer to a data-dependent size. At what ratio of bound to typical size does a sizing node pay for its extra host round trip?
  • The schedule search. Finding an order whose peak fits is a scheduling problem that is hard in general. A plan order from the config, improved greedily, is probably enough for the DAGs this tree has. When is it not?
  • Transport across nodes. The collectives library moves the node row of the worker table ("Workers are a hierarchy"); the nodes row needs a process per node and a clique made from a shared id (wwr_ccl_comm_init_rank_scalable), or MPI beside it. Nothing in the model depends on the choice, and nothing here decides it.
  • Pricing edges across devices. The worker table's "GPUs on one node" row says what a broadcast and a reduce are there, and the plan names each collective's bytes, but the planner chooses no placement, so it prices none. When placement becomes its search, the price of a collective is a measurement per topology (NVLink pairs and PCIe through a host bridge differ by an order on the A6000 node) before it is a formula.
  • Pinned blocks and NUMA. On a multi-socket node, pinned pages on the GPU's own socket copy faster than pages across the interconnect. A pinned block is allocated on its worker's thread, so pinning that thread to the GPU's socket would place the block without the planner knowing. Whether the gap is worth the pinning is a measurement on such a node.

The v0.0.4 layer's, to discuss:

  • Several nodes. The commitment is to one node's GPUs. Keeping several nodes open cheaply is one rule: work on different GPUs communicates only through the collectives layer. B on one host's heap is the first thing that rule would have to answer.
  • What is cleared between replays, and by whom: a reset mode per step (as built), an explicit on_replay, or a node at the loop's entry or end.
